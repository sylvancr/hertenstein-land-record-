At the house

The grey shape is the skyline from this spot, from east through south to west. The curves are the sun's path on midsummer, the equinox, midwinter and the chosen day. Where a curve dips into the grey, the spot is in shade.

How to read it

  • Sun and shade lights the ground for the moment you pick. Blue-grey is in the shadow of a hill or a mountain; dark brown faces away from the sun.
  • Hours of direct sun counts when the sun is above the skyline and shining on the slope, in five-minute steps. Trees and buildings are not in the model, so real shade is greater near them.
  • Sun over the year adds up clear-sky energy on the 21st of each month and compares it with open flat ground at the same place. Over 100% means the slope gains from facing the sun.
  • Steepness uses the canton's farming limits: up to 18% is easy to mow, 18–25% is harder, over 25% is steep ground.

Wetness and frost

  • Where water gathers is a wetness index: it is high where a lot of ground drains through a spot that is nearly flat. Use it to look for damp meadow, springs or places a path will stay muddy. The canton's overland-flow map is the official picture of storm runoff.
  • Hollows and ridges compares each spot with the ground 30 to 90 m around it. Hollows collect cold air on clear, still nights, so late frost hits them first; ridges and the upper slope stay a little warmer.

Where this comes from

Heights come from AWS Terrain Tiles, which in Switzerland carry about 25 to 30 m detail, shifted 3.4 m to match Swiss heights at the lake and the house. That is good for the shape of the slopes and for the skyline out to 20 km, but it smooths terraces, walls, banks and the shoreline. The swisstopo 0.5 m model (swissALTI3D) would sharpen everything and can replace it with one script run (tools/build_terrain.py). Sun positions are calculated for Swiss time; energy is a clear-sky estimate without clouds or haze. Built 21 October 2026.
